  7. 07

    One letter stands apart: ے (bari ye) takes a single shape in words — its final form — and never joins the letter after it. It has no encoded medial form of its own: in the middle of a word the bari ye sound is written exactly like a choti ye (ی), so the two are told apart by sound, not by shape. It never starts a word, and it never appears in an isolated form in a word.
    Reference: Unicode Standard ch. 9 · Kaplan, "The sad sad tale of the BARREE YEH"

  8. 08

    Urdu writes its vowels with four letters: alif, wao, choti ye and bari ye (ا و ی ے), each of them carrying more than one vowel sound. However, note that short vowel sounds are usually unwritten, with implied marks (zabar, zer, pesh), and pronunciation dictated by context. These same four letters also do duty as consonants elsewhere.

  9. 09

    Two special letters require special mention:–Alif madda (آ) — a long alif (ا) with a small superscript alif mark on top, used to write a glottal stop plus long /aː/ in one glyph instead of two (ء + ا). It sits nearly always at the start of a word — آج āj (today), آخر ākhir (last), آسمان āsmān (sky) — and occasionally inside one, as in قرآن.–Hamza (ء) — the glottal-stop letter, the smallest in Urdu script. It carries no vowel of its own, so a short vowel sits on whatever carrier it rides: أَ above alif, إِ below alif, ؤُ on waw, ئِ on ye, or ء alone when there is no carrier. Where Arabic drops the hamza in the middle of words, Urdu keeps it written, which is one of Urdu's distinctive marks. Examples: سائل sā'il (questioner), زائہ.
